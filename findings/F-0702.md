The exact region. Right under "the work that kills.", a row of eight photo tiles, farming, construction, fishing, west africa fishery, heat, air, logging, the pattern, each one holding its own death number: "at least 108,000 construction workers are killed on site every year," "more than 100,000 fishers die on the job every year," "up to 860,000 outdoor workers die each year from the air they breathe," and so on, one after another, tile after tile, as something you scroll and hover through.

What it made me feel, plainly. Uneasy, in a specific way. Not because any single number is false, but because by the third or fourth tile it stopped reading like information and started reading like a highlight reel. It's a gallery. You built a gallery out of how people die at work, and it sits at the top of a page selling a product.

Why a normal person reads it that way. One dead-worker statistic, cited, makes a case. Eight of them, stacked as clickable tiles with photos, dressed as a "the pattern" grand finale ("63 percent of all workplace deaths"), reads like the numbers are there to make the pitch feel weightier, not to inform anyone of anything they can act on. A charity would follow this with what it does about the number. A company selling training footage follows it with a pricing table. Nothing on the page connects "people die doing this" to "and here is how what we sell makes that better." It just sits there as atmosphere, and atmosphere built out of real deaths is the thing that makes a stranger uneasy about a company, even when every individual fact checks out.

The change as the shape of the words. One number, once, tied directly to what the company's footage is supposed to change about that number, would carry the weight honestly. Eight, arranged as a scroll-through gallery with no follow-through claim, carries it as decoration instead.
