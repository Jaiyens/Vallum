The exact thing that happens. Scroll into the hero and a small tag reads "HUMAN CREW" over real film of people working, a rig hand at the pipe, someone on a roof. Keep scrolling (or, with a mouse, just point at the worker's body, the effect is built to arm only when the cursor sits directly on a person) and a circle of reveal opens on the screen exactly where that figure is standing. By the time it clears the frame, the tag has flipped to read "ROBOT TWIN," and a machine occupies the same spot, the same pose, the same shot. Nothing else in the frame changes. Only the worker does. This is the first thing the site does, before the reader has scrolled far enough to reach a single word about consent, pay, or safety.

What it made me feel, plainly. This is worse than any sentence on the old page, because it is not a claim I have to reason my way into, it is a thing I watched happen to a specific person. I hover over a roughneck at the drill pipe, the film slows down and pulls in on him specifically, like the page wants me to look right at him, and then he is gone and a robot is standing where he stood. That is not an argument about the future of physical work. That is a demonstration, aimed at me, of one person being erased and replaced in the exact frame they were just occupying.

Why a normal person reads it that way. The hero copy underneath this was already fixed. "This is the work humans were never meant to do" is gone, and that was the right call. But whoever fixed the sentence left the picture that makes the identical point without needing the words: watch the person, then watch that same person become a robot, on a loop, before a single reassurance has had a chance to land. A farm owner does not need a caption to get what just happened on their screen. Neither would a worker who recognized their own job, or their own kind of work, in the reel being converted. The flinch test is not only "what does the copy say," it is "what did the page just show me," and the page opens by showing a human being overwritten by a machine, live, in place, as its first act.

The would-you-sign test. Picture the farm owner Jay is trying to get access from, or one of the crew he'd film, opening this site before deciding whether to say yes. The very first interactive thing they encounter is themselves, or someone just like them, mid-task, dissolving into a robot double doing the identical motion in the identical spot. Everything that follows, the consent languages, the face blur, the payment, the founder's own honesty about being 18 and alone, has to work uphill against that first thirty seconds. It shouldn't have to.

The change as the shape of the shot, not just the words. Whatever the hero shows needs to earn the same rule the copy now follows: no verdict on the worker, shown or said. A film that establishes real work, and separately establishes that machines are learning to do physical work from footage like it, without ever placing one specific figure in one specific frame and then erasing that exact figure into their own robot double, would say "machines are learning this work" without ever performing "and here is you, gone."
