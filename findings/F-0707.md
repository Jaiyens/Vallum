The one-sentence test. If a friend asked me what this company does, the honest sentence, using only what the page itself says, is: "they send camera crews to film farm workers, fishermen, and construction crews doing the most dangerous parts of their job, so they can sell the footage to companies building robots to do that work instead."

What it made me feel, plainly. That sentence is ugly. Not unfair, ugly. And the reason it's ugly isn't that I'm missing context, it's that it's a fair restatement of the page's own words, "this is the work humans were never meant to do," "we film the people who still do it, so the machines can learn," "licensed to robotics and world-model teams." I didn't have to stretch or guess to get there.

Why a normal person reads it that way. The test isn't whether I can be talked out of that sentence with more detail about consent forms and blurred faces. It's whether that sentence is what a stranger walks away with after one read. It is. The careful, decent-sounding consent section comes after the part that already told me the point of it all is to make these workers' jobs replaceable, and the reassurance doesn't undo the sentence that landed first.

The change as the shape of the words. The page needs to survive its own one-sentence summary. Right now it doesn't, because the plainest true sentence you can pull from it is the one that would make a stranger uneasy.
